As quantum technologies become deployable, robust inference is becoming central. My work combines probabilistic reasoning, optimisation, and physical principles to develop Bayesian frameworks for inference in quantum systems, from finite-data regimes to networked configurations.

I am currently part of the UKRI-JST Quantum Control and Sensing consortium, involving the University of Exeter, the University of Nottingham, King's College London, and the University of Tokyo. I lead the design of Bayesian, symmetry-informed algorithms for experimentally realistic sensing protocols.

Research vision

Measurement is our most direct way of questioning nature. Advancing the foundations of physics therefore requires inference frameworks that remain valid when quantum and relativistic effects are relevant, while emerging quantum technologies that may help us achieve that require robust methods for extracting reliable information when limited data and experimental imperfections cannot be neglected.

My research develops Bayesian approaches to quantum estimation, metrology, and sensing designed specifically for these realistic conditions. Key research themes include:

Quantum inference

  • Global estimation, variational principles, and multiparameter schemes.
  • Symmetry constraints in optimal inference.
  • Quantum metrology beyond idealised regimes.

Quantum technologies

  • Inference-driven design of quantum measurement protocols.
  • Quantum sensing in interferometric, atomic, and networked platforms.

I expect these methods to become increasingly relevant as quantum technologies move from proof-of-principle experiments to distributed and deployable architectures.

On inference, information, and physical law

Beyond my specialised research, my work is guided by the following views.

I was drawn to theoretical physics because I seek a rational account of the natural phenomena that shape our experience, using mathematics to extract meaning from empirical data. I consider time and information among the most fundamental concepts in our theories of nature.

In that context, I see probability theory, understood as an extension of logical reasoning, as the most effective framework for connecting physical theories with experiments, and I believe that progress in understanding often emerges simply from identifying and resolving inconsistencies.

At the same time, I do not see science as purely abstract, but aim for scientific papers that clarify principles and translate them into actionable methodology, which I regard as the primary contribution of my work to society. After all, understanding is the work, and creating is the joy.

Biography

Jesús is a theoretical physicist and quantum scientist working on quantum sensing and metrology, developing optimal measurement algorithms applicable to experimental settings.

After completing the Spanish Baccalaureate, he earned a Licenciatura degree at the Complutense University of Madrid and an MSc at the Institute for Theoretical Physics of Madrid, specialising in particle physics, cosmology, and quantum information theory. In 2019, he completed a PhD on quantum optics, Bayesian metrology, and sensing networks at the University of Sussex.

Since then, his work has focused on developing practically deployable frameworks for quantum metrology under realistic constraints, in collaboration with multiple research organisations. This includes global thermometry, scale estimation, and symmetry-informed sensing validated in cold-atom experiments, together with foundational results in Bayesian multiparameter quantum estimation.

He also has extensive experience in teaching and knowledge transfer in physics, including MSc-level module leadership, and is a Fellow of Advance HE (FHEA).

He currently applies this body of work to problems spanning fundamental physics and emerging quantum technologies.
